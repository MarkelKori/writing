Nick and “Eternity”

Markel Kori / 10 December 2025 / 11 min read

Tonight I crept out with the drug in my hand…

“There you go, you old fossil!”

With those words I swiftly, decisively plunged the needle into my grandfather’s shoulder. Then I pressed the plunger, and the clear liquid began flowing into his veins… He did not wake: earlier, he had drunk tea with an extra dose of sedative in it.

What made me do it? Well, it is a long story, but a very simple one. I love life—I always have, whatever troubles came my way—and my grandfather… Well, I think he loved life too. But until recently the world had harboured a terrible evil, something that made people weaken and fall apart year after year. It had turned his days into meaningless pain, and he had long since given up hoping that anything would change.

There is so much to do in this world. I cannot imagine ever being truly bored. So many astonishingly beautiful places to see, so many fascinating books to read, so many songs, so many people… But of course, when a simple trip to the toilet becomes a test of will, you have little interest in what is happening outside your window.

The generations to come will not even know the name of the disease that afflicted my grandfather, just as few people now know what typhoid fever is. Ageing will belong to the past for ever. That was how it should be, and at last it had come to pass.

Ostap—my old man—had always been sceptical about life extension. Like most people, he insisted that ageing was natural, and therefore necessary. When I pointed out that E. coli and malaria were natural too, he rolled his eyes and refused to discuss it any further.

Ostap had plenty of arguments against a long and happy life, but I thought that, like everyone else, he would forget them once an actual cure appeared. Most of those arguments in favour of ageing and death had nothing to do with reality. They arose for personal reasons, one of them being disbelief that any of this was possible. Hope is frightening when no one offers guarantees. None of us wants to be disappointed.

But even when the vaccines were being given out everywhere, Grandpa had no intention of going to Life Essential.

Sometimes I woke in the night and could not get back to sleep for terror. I saw Ostap burning in a crematorium. His bones and skull consumed by hellish fire. Was this really what, only yesterday, people had thought gave life meaning?

So if Ostap would not go to the vaccine, the vaccine would come to him.

In the morning, as usual, he came down from upstairs in the special lift, then slowly hobbled over to the table, leaning on his stick.

“Morning,” Grandpa drawled with a frown, and it was impossible to tell whether he was wishing us a good one or merely complaining that he had woken up instead of falling asleep for ever.

Ernie, our household robot, had laid on a feast: pancakes with soft cheese, chocolate and apples, sandwiches with lab-grown—but incredibly tasty—sausage and cheese, coffee, tea and water with lemon.

“Delicious!” Dad said, piling a little of everything onto his plate. Mum showed less enthusiasm for food, slowly chewing her crispbread.

“Thank you, Ernie,” I said, as I always did.

Ostap remained silent, gesturing for the robot to serve his food and pour him water. Then he began chewing very slowly, constantly taking sips and coughing from time to time.

“More tea,” Ostap ordered the robot.

Ernie obliged, and unexpectedly the old man said:

“Thank you.”

Mum glanced at her father, but Dad noticed nothing unusual. And I thought: could it be working already?

“When I’ve finished eating, take me to the study,” Ostap told Ernie. “I want to look through the album.”

“Certainly. Enjoy your breakfast,” the robot replied.

It was probably the third time that week Grandpa had wanted to look through the album. I was beginning to wonder whether he really was looking at old photographs, or doing something irrational, as he sometimes had when the fog of delirium took hold of his mind.

So that afternoon I peered through the gap in the doorway. Ostap really was looking at photographs. There he was with Grandma… Sitting on a bench, laughing. I walked away, but something inside me had begun to change.

The next day the old man even smiled—he had not done that in a long time—and two days later he began noticing the first black hairs on his head.

“Well, would you look at that,” he said, sounding almost pleased.

A week later, one morning, Ostap called me upstairs. I went up. He was sitting on the edge of his bed, looking thoughtful, and repeated several times:

“Something bloody strange is going on…”

“What is it?” I asked, but the answer took a long time to come. He drew a deep breath, shrugged, then stood up.

“My knees feel better!”

He began walking around the room. I laughed.

“Well, that’s good, isn’t it?”

Grandpa stopped and slowly nodded.

“But why, Tom?” He called me by my name. “It’s as though I’m recovering from a cold that kept me in bed for… A very long time…” His gaze fixed on a point in space. “Let’s try going downstairs. Without the lift!”

Of course I knew what was happening, but I was amazed all the same.

“Of course!” I said, taking his arm. We went down the stairs together, and as he sat down to breakfast Grandpa began laughing. I had not seen him so happy in a long time.

“I have prepared the selection of photographs you requested,” Ernie told my grandfather. “Would you like to see them after you have eaten?”

Ostap’s expression changed abruptly. He grew serious. More than serious… He grew sombre.

“Yes, Ernie,” Ostap replied, and after a pause added, “Thank you, Ernie.”

For some reason I began to worry. As though something bad was coming.

Unfortunately, I was right.

A few days later the changes in my grandfather’s appearance became unmistakable. He rarely looked in the mirror, but my parents said he seemed ten years younger. They did not know what I had done either.

And then he understood everything.

“Was this you?” he asked sternly when he met me in the corridor. A third of his hair had already regained its colour, and the puffiness in his face had almost gone.

“Was what me?”

“None of that! Tell me straight, Tom, goddammit!” He grabbed my shirt. I had not seen him so angry since I was a child.

“Yes. It was me. I gave you the vaccine.”

Ostap’s eyes locked on mine. I could feel his jaw clenching, feel how strong his hands had become. This ninety-year-old man was quite capable of kicking my arse.

But he let go of my shirt and stepped back.

“You’re an idiot,” Ostap said, and walked away.

I tried calling after him, but he would not turn around, so I decided it was better to leave it alone.

On my way back to my room, I passed Grandpa’s study again. A slideshow was playing on a loop on the screen. Restored old photographs: him and Grandma. Their wedding, their trip to the Poltava region where Grandpa had grown up, the way they had grown old together… But had not died together. Sadie had gone much earlier, when I was still a child.

Now I understood. I realised why, all this time, he had not wanted to stop ageing, had not wanted to turn away from death.

My stomach twisted, and it felt as though something had struck me in the head. Had I… Had I been wrong? I did not know… I was not sure. But I thought… I ought to apologise to him as soon as possible.

I ran back and up the stairs, but Grandpa was not in his bedroom. Nor in the living room, nor any of the other rooms. I looked out of the window at the streets of Jersey, but Ostap was nowhere to be seen.

My parents were angry too. That did not surprise me. When I asked where Grandpa was, they said it was none of my business. But if that was their answer, it meant he was all right. And that was enough for me.

I went on doing what I had done before. Reading, painting, taking long walks. I kept wondering whether I had done the right thing, but could find no answer.

Some time passed. I received a call about the purchase of a painting.

“I think Sea of Winds would make a wonderful addition to my collection,” said Nick, my new admirer.

“Would you like the virtual version or the physical one?” I asked.

“The physical one.”

“Good. You can come to my office at four today, if that suits you.”

“How about five?”

“That works.”

“Agreed! See you then.”

Nick hung up.

By ten to five I was waiting for him in my office with two empty glasses and a nearly full bottle of brandy. He arrived a little later than promised—at fourteen minutes past five.

Nick wore a formal, old-fashioned suit, his short, jet-black hair slicked down. His white teeth seemed to reflect more light than was physically possible.

“I’m so glad to meet you at last.” He shook my hand firmly.

“Likewise,” I replied. “Please, sit down. Would you care for some brandy?”

“Wonderful idea,” he said, taking a seat.

I soon returned with the glasses and settled onto the sofa opposite him.

“Is it my imagination, or is the painting behind you damaged?” Nick asked. I set my glass on the coffee table and turned to examine Eternity. I could see nothing wrong, and said so.

“Forgive me. My mistake.”

I noticed a bead of sweat on his forehead.

“Is something wrong?” I asked.

“Everything’s fine. It’s simply been a busy day. Let’s have a drink.”

“Gladly.”

We drank.

“So, you were interested in Sea of Winds?” I asked.

“No,” Nick said shortly.

I was taken aback.

“Um… Perhaps I’ve mixed something up. Which painting did you want to buy?”

“None of them, Tom.” Nick’s smile slowly widened, and I began to feel dizzy.

I reached my right hand towards the bracelet on my left wrist to call the police, but I was too late. My limbs went limp, and everything sank into darkness.

I should not have put my brandy down on that table…

We were in a boat. I knew it before I even raised my head.

“I never liked your daubs,” Nick said, working the oars.

“Have we known each other long, then?” I asked, sitting up and scanning the horizon.

We were in the middle of a wide lake. Not another soul in sight, and the sun seemed to have just slipped below the horizon. He could easily hit me over the head and throw me overboard.

“Don’t worry, I’ll still buy one of your paintings,” Nick said. “Eternity, perhaps…”

I studied his face. Only now did I realise how strongly it reminded me of someone I had once known…

“Ostap,” I said at last. He did not look quite as he had in the old photographs… Or perhaps I had simply never looked at them closely enough.

“Of course it’s me. Who else but family would support the efforts of foolish youngsters?”

“Ostap!” I repeated, unable to believe my eyes. He laughed, and I threw myself at him in an embrace.

“You’ll tip the boat over!” he said, still laughing.

I let go of Ostap, but could not stop looking at him. He had recovered completely, becoming a young man of twenty.

“I got my revenge,” Ostap said.

“Fair enough. I’m so glad to see you,” I said. “How does it feel?”

He shrugged.

“Not bad.”

“Not bad?!” I repeated.

Ostap roared with laughter.

“Wonderful, of course! Incredible. Like a fairy tale. Is that what you wanted to hear?”

“Yes… Exactly! So I did the right thing after all, sticking that needle into you?”

“I’m not going to praise you, Tom. But… I have to say yes. I’m glad it happened.”

We sat in silence for a while.

“I don’t even know whether I ought to apologise,” I said.

“Neither do I,” Grandpa replied. “But probably not. I… Let me try to explain. You probably don’t remember her…”

He drew a deep breath and closed his eyes.

“I dreamed of seeing her again,” he continued, opening his eyes. “My Sadie. Your grandmother. I hoped that when I died, all those stories about life after death would turn out to be true. And she would be there, as beautiful as ever… She would hold out her little hands to me, and I would take them and kiss her…”

Ostap fell silent. A tear slid down his cheek.

“That’s why I didn’t want to grow young again. I wanted it all to be over at last, so I could be with her again. That’s all. When I realised what you’d done, that dream was destroyed.”

I did not know what to say.

“I’m sorry,” I said.

“Don’t be silly. Deep down we all know that heaven, just like hell, is a foolish idea. It hurts people unbearably to admit that the ones they love are truly gone.”

“And you… Have you admitted it?”

He smiled a little.

“While I was away, I read some scientific literature. You know, there was a time when people couldn’t have imagined that a single syringe could turn the bag of bones I used to be into a handsome young man.”

I smiled and wiped away a tear that had escaped my eye unnoticed.

“I’ve been thinking about that for a long time,” I said. “If science has spent centuries proving it can do the impossible, why shouldn’t it happen again?”

“Exactly, son,” Ostap replied. “The day will come when there is no evil left in the world. When we bring back everyone who didn’t live to see today—the age in which ageing has been defeated. I’m sorry I didn’t understand that before.”

“I’m sorry you’ll have to wait so long.”

“I’ve got plenty of time.” He smiled again.

“An unlimited amount, to be precise!”

“Exactly. So tomorrow we’re flying to Cairo.”

“Cairo?”

“I’m ninety and I still haven’t seen the pyramids!”

We laughed.

“Skydiving’s booked for Friday,” the old man added. Though the words no longer suited him.

He looked my age: every wrinkle and spot gone, his stoop straightened, the strength restored to his muscles… But most importantly, his mind had grown young too—and happy again.

We spent the evening watching the stars.

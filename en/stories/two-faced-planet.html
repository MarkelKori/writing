The Two-Faced Planet

Markel Kori / 24 November 2020 / 4 min read

Working as a porter on a planet with laser weapons and flying cars is a thankless business.

Admittedly, flying cars were banned long ago, and not because they were particularly dangerous or difficult to use. No, it was all because of that damned wall running from pole to pole. In a car you could fly straight over it—the military would have got tired of shooting down everyone brave enough to try. So now we were back on the ground.

I used to think dualism was dull, childish stuff. But now, with those bastards putting up an endless barrier and dividing an entire planet in half to settle their political problems, I was beginning to believe that good and evil were all there was.

“What are you standing around for, eh? There’s another couple of dozen crates to go.”

My workmate Jim spat on the ground and carried a crate into the warehouse. Only then did I realise how long I had been staring into the distance, through the thick, sturdy metal bars separating my half of the planet from what lay beyond the barrier. It was hard to tear my eyes away from those endless spaces behind the bars… They were my only salvation: everything at once so close, so immovable, so out of reach…

But I had to swallow those thoughts again and get back to work. If the boss saw me standing idle for so long, he might start up his old tune about not paying me today. “Maybe you’ll work harder tomorrow.” There was barely enough money for food as it was, damn him…

I lifted a crate. It seemed much heavier than before, probably because I had stopped for a break. Still, I had been doing this job for years, and my muscles had grown strong with practice. Though I could still remember my first day—I spent a week laid up afterwards, aching all over.

Van to warehouse, van to warehouse… Five trips or so, until two boys came up to me. They looked about thirteen.

“Mister, can we help you?”

I looked at them. Their arms were a quarter the thickness of mine, and they were half my height. Each crate probably weighed almost as much as they did. They wore torn cast-offs, several old jackets full of holes, with newspaper stuffed under their shirts for warmth.

“No, boys. This isn’t work for you,” I said, heading back to the van.

“It’s just… We need money…” one of them whined pitifully.

“What, there’s no work for you?”

“Nobody takes children. They tell us to stay at home.”

I cracked my knuckles. Pity got the better of me. I rummaged in my pockets and found a few coins—the last money I had. I kept the larger ones and tossed the small change to the boys.

“And don’t let me see you here again.”

They nodded eagerly and ran off.

I laughed and went on working, thinking about the place I lived in. By nightfall Jim and I were finished. The van driver returned and paid us, then got into the vehicle and drove towards the barrier. We stood watching him.

“Want one?” Jim asked, taking out his cigarettes.

“You know me.”

“All right.” He took just one, put the rest back in his pocket and lit it. “Think they’ll let it through?”

I shrugged.

“I think so.”

“What’ll you bet?”

“A bottle of beer, if anything.”

“Done.” Jim’s moustache rose over his smile. He took a drag and blew the smoke towards the ground.

The van receded along the dirt road towards the wall. Before long the red light at the checkpoint turned green, and the gates opened briefly. The whole time they were open, several soldiers scanned the area from the towers, weapons at the ready. The turrets came alive too, and cameras trained themselves on the van and in our direction. The vehicle crossed to the other side. The gates closed at once, and the wall became utterly impassable again.

“Lucky. Won’t keep doing that for long. Sooner or later he’ll let something slip about life on the other side, and they’ll bump him off,” Jim said.

He was probably right. A few times I had seen drivers like that eliminated as soon as we finished unloading. They knew too much. Life on the other side was very different, and one of the authorities’ main tasks now was to make sure nobody knew that anything better was possible.

Our half of the planet is perfect. Our life is wonderful. Beyond the barrier—nothing…

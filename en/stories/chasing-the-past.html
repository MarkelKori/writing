Chasing the Past

Markel Kori / 4 June 2025 / 8 min read

The past had always chased me.

Or was I the one chasing the past? To be honest, I still had not decided.

Outside the window it was early summer. The trees had grown lush, fashionable hairdos, and the flowerbeds in the little square glowed with all manner of fragrant blooms. It was a cloudless, windless day. The Brutalist building stirred memories of my childhood, and of times before I was even born.

A thought occurred to me: how long would our new government take to tear that building down and put a high-tech tower in its place?

I had always been a fan of skyscrapers, yet remembering the little cottage my grandfather built more than seventy years ago still filled me with warmth and comfort.

In any case, the building now sported a huge purple banner that read “VIRTUALITY”. The phrase “Dissolve into a higher reality” flashed through my mind, and I raised my eyebrows involuntarily, feeling contempt and disappointment well up inside me.

My thoughts were abruptly interrupted by a tall figure moving with what seemed to me too much assurance for a woman. Only the handbag on her left shoulder, and the hand holding it, gave her away.

“Hello, Ted. You do know you’re not allowed to bring your own drinks in here?” She smiled almost imperceptibly and pointed to my water-filled biobubble. That rule had once existed in many establishments, but it disappeared when we entered the post-scarcity era.

“Rita!” I leapt eagerly from the table, spilling half my soup. For a couple of seconds I stood there in bewildered silence, and she immediately looked away, as though she did not even want to see me.

“Let me shake your hand,” I said for some reason, and our palms met briefly. “You look even better than you did twenty-seven years ago!”

The corners of her mouth lifted slightly. Of course, you idiot, I thought. Rejuvenation and healing are available to almost everyone now.

“I still can’t get used to the way you always remember how many years it’s been,” she said.

“Twenty-seven years, three months and, I think, thirteen days since we met. Though I might be wrong about the days!”

The faint smile stayed on her face for only a second before disappearing, and she took a few steps towards the exit.

“Have a good day!” I called after her. She may have said something in return, though I could not make it out. I wanted to ask whether she was planning to visit the NCVM—the National Centre for Virtual Migration—but there was no time. I suspected she had been going there for a while, since I had not seen her for an entire year.

It was awful. How could I, a forty-five-year-old doctor of biological sciences, behave so absurdly?

I sat down again and took some tissues out of my briefcase, but before I could do anything a robot rolled over, extended an arm fitted with already-soapy sponges, and wiped the table.

I took a deep breath. Every time Rita and I ran into each other, I turned into an eighteen-year-old boy. Just as naive, just as awkward.

Somewhere inside me lived the hope of returning to a time when life had been full of fun and… Foolishness, I suppose.

It felt as though I had never known how to let go. Never known what it meant to accept something and leave it in the past. I had found my purpose in fighting the great problems—poverty, war, hunger, even death itself. But now that all those problems were solved and nobody needed my knowledge of biology, I had no idea what to do.

Most of the people I knew had disappeared into the NCVM. And, as always, I refused to accept it. I had hoped people would choose the real world. For years I had hoped that utopia would come when everyone on Earth became psychologically healthy. But how had I failed to see that human defence mechanisms would win? Escaping was so much easier than finding a new way to live, a new purpose. People had always chosen the easiest path, even when it was the wrong one.

But these days, what was right was no longer decided by people. It was decided by the Great Intelligence…

I pushed aside the biobubble and what was left of my soup, and took my notebook from my briefcase. The last page was devoted to goals I might set myself in this strange new world, where there was no longer any need to work or create.

Learn to surf. Learn to skateboard. Master at least one board game. Take up the piano again and give a concert…

And once more a memory struck me: the same sort of notebook, a page of goals, only I was thirteen at most…

Find a cure for Grandpa’s illness. Understand how to bring people back to life. Find a way to travel faster than light…

I closed the notebook and shoved it back into my briefcase, hoping never to see it again. No hobby could replace the great purpose that had kept me afloat all these years as the people I loved left me—whether by their own choice or at death’s bidding. I had immersed myself ever more deeply in the science of life, searching for new cures… And then, in just two and a half years, artificial intelligence had done it all for me.

Well. I had had enough.

“The effect is achieved primarily through a vast range of electromagnetic waves at different frequencies passing through your brain. We also administer an additional dose of dimethyltryptamine…”

I stopped the instructor with a gesture.

“Thank you, but I understand the general principle.”

At the entrance I had been offered a choice between a robot assistant and a human being. I chose the latter.

“Shall we move on to the paperwork, then?” he asked.

I nodded and smiled a little. It was probably a very unconvincing smile. He handed me a pen and a sheet headed “NCVM”.

Fifteen minutes later I was lying in a soft white chair while robots adjusted the equipment around me.

“We’ll run a simulation based on the particular characteristics of your nervous system. You can choose the duration of your stay on the touchpad. Bear in mind that one subjective hour there is equivalent to roughly ten objective minutes here,” the young instructor went on. “One of the most important settings is the administration of the memory-island inhibitor. It determines whether, while in virtuality, you’ll remember your real life and identity.”

I enabled the inhibitor.

“To begin with, I want to set the parameters myself. I don’t want them selected automatically,” I said.

“Of course. You need only think about what you’d like to see, and the computer will work out which simulation to create.”

“Thank you, doc.”

The young man smiled.

“See you later, I suppose?” he asked.

“We’ll see.”

Without looking at the touchpad, I selected a random immersion time and pressed start.

What we call reality suddenly became unreal. In the very first instant I felt, with absolute clarity, that my entire previous life had been a lie. But now, when nothing remained of the old world except blank spaces, true Reality began to show through between them.

I had grown much smaller. Or had I always been this size? I must be starting to forget. But… Forget what?

I pulled off the warm, soft blanket and got out of bed. The house was a little cool, but I knew it would be hot outside.

Strangely, there was nobody in the room. Nobody in the adjoining rooms either—only bags and scattered belongings. So I went into the hall, slipped on my rubber sandals, opened the great heavy door, took one step down—and found myself on the grass.

Dew soon wet my feet, and the smell of flowers struck me with a sharp rush of pleasure. Only one thing troubled me: where was everyone?

I walked across the garden towards the compost heap and the old apple tree. Then I spotted my father, who had unintentionally hidden himself among the bushes and was eating plums. He had always seemed so grown-up and powerful to me, but now, for some reason, I saw a young man—black-haired, lightly stubbled, smooth-skinned.

We exchanged a few words, but I stopped paying attention when my grandfather appeared from behind the gazebo. For some reason he too looked nothing like the hundred-year-old man I was used to seeing, but much younger! He smiled and waved with his left hand, because he was holding some tool in his right. He was always repairing something.

The colours around me were growing richer and richer. Strange. Was I ill?

I think it is time to go to the gazebo. My little sister will be there, and Mum and Grandma. I love my sister very much, even if she will not take me out with her friends. I am frightened of Mum and Grandma, though I really love them too. They often make delicious things. For breakfast today there will probably be curd cheese with jam. I do not like curd cheese, but I love jam!

I do not know why, but before going into the gazebo I ran over to Grandpa and hugged him tightly. He laughed and patted my back.

For some reason I wanted to cry with joy. But why? I never hugged Grandpa… I must definitely be ill! Or…

For a moment I thought I had finally caught the past and won our game of chase. But the past wriggled free to remind me of something…

Before I pressed start, I had glimpsed out of the corner of my eye how long I would have to spend here.

On the one hand, it frightened me. So many years of my life would go to waste. I would lie sleeping in a capsule… What if Rita wanted to see me? What if…

On the other hand, I no longer believed anything good was waiting for me in that world. For years I had fought for happiness, yet remained alone. Now, at last, I was home…

I wonder why I still remember the future? A temporary fluctuation in the inhibitor supply, probably… In any case, it will pass soon. And I still have time to think…

What could be better than going back to childhood, when everyone was alive and the grass and leaves were truly green? When butterflies flew in the grove and my grandfather sang along with the birds… When I rode a tiny bicycle with four wheels and thought it would all go on for ever.

These thoughts were breaking my heart. I wanted to bring them back for real, not deceive myself. Perhaps I had made a mistake, and ought to stop all this… Or…

Or perhaps I simply need to eat more of Grandma’s jam!
